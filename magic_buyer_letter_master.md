# Master letter

This is the voice. The generator reads everything above the `---` line as the
tone and shape to match; the notes below it are for you, not the model.

Placeholders in `{braces}` are filled per buyer. Edit freely — whatever you write
here is what the letters, emails, texts and call scripts will sound like.

Dear {owner_name},

I am a real estate agent here in {city}, and I am writing to you about one particular buyer rather than about your house.

Their name is {buyer_nickname}, and here is where they stand:

  • {readiness}
  • {condition_tolerance}
  • {motivation}

Their budget is {budget_range}, and yours is the kind of home they keep asking me about. {why_area}

I want to be straightforward about why you are getting this. I am not sending this to the whole neighborhood, and I am not asking you to list your house. I have one buyer, your home fits what they are looking for, and I would rather ask you directly than wonder.

So the only question is this: if the right offer came along, would you be open to a conversation about it?

If the answer is no, that is a completely fine answer, and you will not hear from me about it again. If the answer is maybe, I can tell you what a home like yours is worth right now, with no obligation and no listing conversation attached.

You can reach me directly at {agent_cell}.

Sincerely,

{agent_name}
{agent_brokerage}
DRE #{agent_dre}

---

## Why each part is there

Change anything you like — these notes just explain the reasoning so you can edit
with your eyes open.

**"about one particular buyer rather than about your house"** — the first line
has to earn the second. Every homeowner in Palos Verdes has thrown away a
hundred "thinking of selling?" postcards. This one says something different in
its opening breath: it is about a person, not a solicitation.

**The three bullets** — the same three the email uses, in the same order:
readiness, condition tolerance, motivation. They are what make the buyer *real*
rather than a device for starting a conversation. A homeowner can tell the
difference immediately.

**"Their budget is..."** — round numbers, always. `$2M and $2.4M`, never
`$1,997,000`. Precision on a number you cannot guarantee reads as a sales tactic.

**"I am not sending this to the whole neighborhood"** — say it plainly. If it is
true, it is the most disarming sentence in the letter. Keep the list small enough
that it stays true.

**"I would rather ask you directly than wonder"** — this is the whole pitch in
nine words. It is a human reason, not a marketing one.

**The one question** — one ask, and a small one. Not "list with me." Not "call
for a free valuation." Just: would you be open to a conversation. Anything bigger
gives them a reason to say no.

**"that is a completely fine answer"** — giving people an easy exit is what makes
the maybes answer. Someone who feels cornered does not reply at all; someone who
feels free to decline sometimes doesn't.

**The valuation offer** — the ladder for the maybes. No obligation, no listing
conversation. Whoever takes it has raised their hand.

**Direct cell, signed by a person, DRE number** — a licensed agent, reachable,
accountable. Not a brand.

## What must never go in

The fair housing screen enforces this, but it is worth knowing why:

- **Never who the buyer is** — no family, no children, no age, no religion,
  no nationality, no marital status. "A young family relocating" is a fair
  housing violation, and it is the single easiest one to write by accident.
  Describe what they want to *buy*, never who they *are*.
- **Never who the neighborhood suits** — no "great for families", no "safe
  area", no "good schools". That is steering, and it is the second easiest one
  to write by accident.
- **Never a promise** — no guaranteed price, no "I have a buyer who will pay
  X". You do not know what they will offer until they offer it.
- **Never urgency** — no "act now", no "last chance". It reads as pressure and
  invites exactly the reply you do not want.

## A note on the ask

Jimmy's version asks the reader to forward the email. That works because the
email goes to people who already know you. The letter is different: it goes to a
stranger, about their own home, so the ask has to be smaller and the exit has to
be easier. Same campaign, different door.
